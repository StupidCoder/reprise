Putting Sonic on a Nintendo handheld carried an unusual emotional charge. In a December 2001 conversation with Shigeru Miyamoto, Yuji Naka described both excitement and anxiety about reaching Nintendo's audience. Sonic could no longer rely on being the obvious purchase for owners of Sega hardware. Sonic Advance had to make its case as a game on someone else's machine, while preserving the qualities that made its star recognisable.

Two-dimensional play was a deliberate attraction. Naka explained that it allowed precise decisions about where to put objects and how to adjust a course, and said he enjoyed making Advance. That perspective gives the return to a side-on view a positive creative rationale. The appeal was the ability to shape encounters carefully, with the relationship between the player's movement and the surrounding terrain available for close adjustment.

The project also began an enduring collaboration with Dimps. Asked about that relationship in 2010, Sonic Team's Takashi Iizuka traced it back to Sonic Advance and praised Dimps' design experience, technical ability and working methods. He described a partnership concentrated largely on handheld, two-dimensional Sonic games. Advance thus represents more than a change of console: it was the beginning of a continuing production relationship around a particular kind of Sonic adventure.

The finished game offered Sonic, Tails, Knuckles and Amy, with abilities that changed how the same courses felt. Ricardo Torres' contemporary GameSpot review singled out both the differing approaches to obstacles and Amy's hammer. He also noted that the sprites followed the characters' Sonic Adventure appearances. The combination connected several eras at once: familiar side-scrolling play, a broader playable cast and the visual identities of the contemporary console games. Returning to two dimensions did not require returning to exactly the same Sonic.

Our examination of the original GBA cartridge reveals a substantial new technical construction, separate from the Game Gear engines. Its terrain describes floor heights, wall widths and surface angles, with two collision layers. Rings, enemies and other interactive objects have their own compressed placement data. These structures give precise meaning to the apparently simple picture of a character running across a landscape: the game must know which surface supports the player, which direction it faces and which objects belong nearby.

The connection to Nintendo hardware also extended beyond the handheld screen. Advance's Tiny Chao Garden could exchange creatures with the GameCube through a cable. Naka recalled wanting to use that connection as soon as he heard about it, then discovering that implementation was harder than expected. Miyamoto, in turn, said Sega's active testing had helped Nintendo. The former competitors were now solving a shared practical problem, with a small virtual creature travelling between their games.

Contemporary reception caught some of the tension in this new home. Torres welcomed the presentation and familiar play but criticised hazards arriving from offscreen and moments that encouraged caution rather than speed. That is a useful counterweight to treating the release only as a symbolic reconciliation. Behind the novelty of Sonic on Nintendo was a concrete design challenge: make fast, readable platforming work on a handheld, for several characters, while connecting it to a wider world of games.
