A handheld Sonic game could introduce more than another collection of zones. Triple Trouble gave the series Fang, the hat-wearing treasure hunter also known as Nack. His arrival is a useful way into the game's story: by 1994, the portable adventures had room to contribute their own characters and situations. This was a branch of Sonic with an identity worth expanding, and a new rival offered another kind of trouble to put in the player's path.

Artist Shinichi Higashi, later known as Touma, recalled admiring Sonic in shop displays before joining Sega. In a 2026 interview with Jack Yarwood, he discussed the influence of Tom and Jerry and Bruce Timm's economical Batman designs. Fang began as a jerboa-like figure with large feet, intended to read at a smaller size than Sonic.

The personality changed during development. Higashi remembered an initially harsher villain with a futuristic gun, followed by revisions to both appearance and weapon as the character became more comical. He tentatively connected that change to Knuckles' arrival in Sonic 3. He credited Tadashi Ihoroi with Fang's personality and backstory: an impatient, foolish troublemaker with an endearing side. That account describes an evolving role, rather than supporting a simple claim that a gun was removed for censorship.

The finished game's structure gives its rival a place outside the ordinary succession of acts. Our examination of the cartridge distinguishes an on-foot special stage, a flight stage and a separate Nack encounter following a flight. These are individually managed scenes, rather than one bonus format repeated without change. The different modes create room for a character to appear as an event, with a rhythm separate from running through the main landscapes.

Those landscapes have their own elaborate interruptions. Sunset Park's third act becomes a train ride, and the recovered player logic includes dedicated handling for the train, tubes, bubbles, boards and other temporary situations. Sonic and Tails also bring different movement possibilities, including Tails' flight. The research makes clear how much of the adventure consists of adapting the player's usual movement to a particular set piece. New scenery is often accompanied by new rules for passing through it.

There is continuity beneath that expansion. Triple Trouble belongs to the engine family examined in Sonic 2, sharing graphics compression and related sound and object systems. Its object instruction vocabulary grows from ten commands to eighteen, adding facilities such as loops and jumps. Those are findings from the cartridges, not testimony about Aspect's internal tools. They nevertheless show a practical form of development: retain useful machinery, extend its expressive range and revise the parts needed for another adventure.

The result combines two kinds of invention. Artists refined a newcomer until his silhouette and temperament suited the cast, while the game gave familiar heroes a succession of unusual situations. Fang's development is especially revealing because the revisions concerned what sort of rival he should be, not just how he should look. Triple Trouble's portable scale did not prevent it from asking that larger character-design question.
