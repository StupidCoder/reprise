Draw a line and watch a character follow it. Spy Mouse's central gesture had already helped make Firemint famous: in Flight Control, a finger traced the routes that brought aircraft safely home. Moving that idea into a house full of cats changed its meaning. A line became a plan for a small adventure, with cheese to collect, danger to anticipate and a hero whose escape you wanted to see.

According to studio head Rob Murray, the team explored many prototypes after Flight Control, including several built around line drawing. They kept returning to stealth. Over more than two years, that idea grew through cheese gathering, gadgets, enemy types and individually designed levels. Murray stressed the amount of authored content: each new place was meant to introduce something worth discovering, rather than merely rearrange the same challenge.

The ambition went beyond finding another use for a successful control scheme. Speaking to Pocket Gamer at GDC 2011, Murray described wanting a character with the kind of broad appeal Mario enjoyed. The phone was central to that ambition. Drawing a path could be understood immediately, while difficult levels and optional depth gave experienced players more to explore. Accessibility was meant to open the door to a substantial game, not determine how demanding it could become.

The visual identity helped give that ambition a face. Artist Rich McBride identifies style development, backgrounds, the world map and interface among his responsibilities on Spy Mouse. The game's presentation extends well beyond the rooms where you collect cheese. Our examination finds introductions that pick out characters with spotlights, animated information panels and transitions that close around mouse or cat silhouettes. Those small performances make moving between challenges feel like part of the adventure.

Even the name had to introduce the idea quickly. The game was shown before release as Agent Squeak, but Murray later explained that the character's name and the final product title were separate questions. Spy Mouse told an unfamiliar player more about what awaited them. The older spelling also survives behind the scenes: the supplied executable contains development paths naming the project AgentSqueek. A public identity changed while traces of the working project remained.

By release, Firemint itself had changed ownership. EA announced its acquisition in May 2011, and Spy Mouse launched that August as the studio's first game published by EA Mobile. Murray told GameSpot that the publisher supplied resources and reach for Firemint's ambitions. He was also thinking beyond launch: continued additions could keep a game alive, although producing another update immediately after finishing a demanding project created its own pressure. The initial release offered 72 levels across six worlds.

The care behind the simple gesture is visible in the original game. Drawn routes are smoothed, kept clear of walls and snapped towards useful targets. In the first level, the demonstrating finger follows a route and pauses stored in the map, then disappears when the player starts drawing. The game can teach through the same action it asks you to perform. That is the enduring connection to Flight Control: a movement of your hand becomes an intention the game understands, and watching it unfold is part of the pleasure.
