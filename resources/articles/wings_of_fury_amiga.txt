Landing is one of Wings of Fury's most memorable battles. The carrier that supplies your weapons and repairs is also a narrow target you must approach correctly after a sortie. The game wraps that repeated journey in an apparently simple sideways view, giving the flight of one aircraft enough weight to sustain an entire campaign. Bringing it to the Amiga meant preserving that feeling while rebuilding the machinery beneath it.

Steve Waldo created the Apple II original. Broderbund's manual introduces a computer-science student with a fine-art minor, fascinated by animation and Second World War aircraft. It traces his interest in programming to a school that removed games from its computer centre. Making moving pictures became a pleasure in its own right, and Wings of Fury joined that fascination to the subject of the Hellcat.

The Amiga version came from Unlimited Software, with Colin Fox programming and John Boechler creating graphics. In interviews reported by Game Not Over, Fox described it as his first commercial Amiga project. He had years of hobby programming behind him, but this was a different kind of responsibility: making an existing game feel right on another machine.

Fox recalled receiving none of the original development files. Instead, he played the Apple II game repeatedly and spoke with Waldo while rebuilding it. He estimated roughly ten months for the work. His stated aim was to stay close to the original play while giving the presentation an Amiga identity. This was a reconstruction of an experience, rather than a straightforward transfer of the Apple II program.

Even the sound has a small human mystery. Asked about the soldiers' recorded cries, Fox remembered Boechler supplying them; Boechler thought they sounded like Fox. The disagreement, preserved in the same interview report, is more revealing than a confidently assigned credit would be. Familiar details can outlive the team's agreement about who made them.

The Amiga program exposes the depth beneath the shooting. Our research finds a flight model with speed, lift and turning behaviour, alongside separate systems for weapons, enemy aircraft, island targets and the carrier's machinery. A distant view changes how the action is presented, while briefings and promotions connect the sorties. The important continuity is the return to the ship: attacking successfully is only part of the task if you cannot bring the aircraft back.

There is also a surprising limit to that campaign's ending. In the examined Amiga version, the final map leads back into a repeating sequence of the last three maps, with further promotions and fireworks. It does not conclude with a conventional final scene. The war becomes a continuing test of competence, returning the player to the same essential demands: judge the approach, choose the weapon, keep the aircraft alive, and land.
