Beach Volley spends time introducing its world before it asks you to win a point. There is a trophy, a colourful title sequence, comic-strip storytelling and voices. Between the matches, travel gives the competition the shape of a little adventure. Ocean France's game treats a simple sport as the centre of a much larger cartoon presentation.

Studio head Marc Djan described founding Ocean France from scratch to handle Ocean's 16-bit development. Early assignments included Operation Wolf and Dragon Ninja. In his interview with Codetapper, he explained that delays between projects eventually encouraged him to seek suitable arcade games himself and ask Ocean to obtain the rights. He also identified Beach Volley among projects based on ideas from coin-operated games, rather than presenting it as a named licensed conversion.

Djan's background helps explain the emphasis on presentation. He had studied architecture at the Beaux-Arts school in Paris and spoke of games needing to be both playable and beautiful. His role crossed direction, production and art. That account does not identify who proposed every scene, but it makes the studio's interest in animation and visual finish understandable as a deliberate part of its work.

The Amiga credits reflect a team effort: Djan on design, Michel Janicki on programming, Michèle Bacqué, Philippe Dessoly and Pierre-Eric Loriaux on graphics, and Jean Baudlot on music. The result has several kinds of drawing to sustain: the comic introduction, the players in motion, the wide courts and the scenes surrounding the competition. Their shared tone matters as much as any single background.

Our examination of the original disk shows how substantial that surrounding material is. The introduction, match, score display, travel and finale are separate program sections, with their own pictures and music. The introduction contains seven comic pages and six recorded voices. Travel has its own map and animation files. These are authored parts of the game, loaded as the journey proceeds, rather than incidental screens wrapped around one self-contained match.

The court artwork also does more than provide a static postcard. Each of the eight backgrounds is wider than the visible playing area, allowing the view to scroll across it. The game writes the score into that background picture. Palettes and music help distinguish venues while the competition remains recognisable. Looking behind the scenes reveals a practical way to give the same sport a succession of different settings without needing eight different games.

Beach Volley's development story is clearest in that balance. A compact sporting activity carries a travelling show of drawings, voices and music; the presentation makes each new match feel like another stop rather than a reset. We have less direct testimony about its day-to-day production than about Ocean France as a studio. What survives in the game itself is a vivid example of the visual ambition Djan described, extending well beyond the court.
