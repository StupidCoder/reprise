Wizball makes its scenery part of the reward. At first the landscape seems drained of life; collecting paint gradually gives it colour. Shooting is only one part of the job. You must also learn to steer an awkward bouncing body, look after a smaller companion and bring something useful back from the chaos. Its strange collection of ideas has a surprisingly personal history.

Jon Hare and Chris Yates had already worked together as musicians before making games. Hare told Talkin'Games that writing and polishing music together taught them how to collaborate. Wizball followed Twister and Parallax, drawing on their enthusiasm for Defender and Archer Maclean's Dropzone. An arcade shooter's movement and danger provided the starting point for something less conventional.

The colour idea came from Hare's studies in theatre design, particularly lighting. He remembered becoming interested in building colours from red, green and blue, then imagining a landscape that players could colour in. Paint droplets and a laboratory gave that idea a playable form. The restoration was becoming a purpose for the shooting.

The characters arrived along the way. In Hare's account, adding life to the laboratory led to a cat emerging from the small satellite and a wizard from the larger ball. Yates's cat Nifta was around the house where they worked. The name followed the characters. Elsewhere Hare credits Yates with the bouncing ball and orbiting companion, and himself with paint and colour restoration: a shared design assembled from complementary ideas.

Our examination of the C64 game shows how neatly that premise fits the machine. The landscapes are built from reusable blocks of characters. Painting does not require storing another complete picture for every stage of restoration: the game changes the colours used to display the scenery. Progress can therefore transform an entire landscape while much of its underlying graphic data stays the same. That is an observation about the finished game, rather than a claim that the hardware limitation originally suggested the idea.

The companion is more than a decorative second sprite. In the original rules it normally follows an earlier position of the wizard, then responds to directional control when steered. Its paint-catching duties give the player another moving part to manage. Its vulnerability also matters: losing it interrupts the work of restoring the world. The familiar shooter arrangement of a hero and a supporting weapon becomes a small partnership with different responsibilities.

That partnership gives Wizball its particular rhythm. Destruction creates an opportunity to collect; collecting produces a visible change to the place you inhabit. The little laboratory scene gives the abstract balls identities, while the colour system makes progress readable without a long explanation. A bouncing shooter, an interest in stage lighting and an unlikely feline helper ended up belonging to the same world.
