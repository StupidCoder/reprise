Fort Apocalypse sends a helicopter somewhere a helicopter has no business going: deep underground, through tunnels crowded with prisoners, machinery and hostile aircraft. Steve Hales remembered the idea beginning with a dream, then developing through his collaboration with Synapse founder Ihor Wolosenko. He rejected the familiar description of the game as a Choplifter imitation, recalling that an early version already existed before he interrupted it to finish Slime.

That unlikely setting was deliberate. Hales wanted an experience he could not have outside a computer, rather than a realistic flying exercise. Making it playable proved harder than imagining it. He singled out the enemy helicopters' behaviour and the difficulty tuning as challenges; complaints about the version shown at CES led to changes to the opening level.

Scale was another compromise. In his Halcyon Days interview, Hales recalled an ambition of fifty levels, reduced to two by the planned 16 KB cartridge. He spent time trying to squeeze more into the available space. In a later interview he distinguished that ambition from finished work: four layouts had actually reached the game, with more on paper, and two were dropped after difficult playtests.

The C64 edition was Joe Vierra's conversion of Hales's Atari game. Hales later described most of its code as his own, suggesting a substantial transfer of the existing design rather than an unrelated remake. His recollections differ on whether the conversion took roughly a week or a month, so the precise duration is uncertain. What remains clear is the division of credit: Hales created the game, and Vierra brought it to Commodore's machine.

The C64 program still shows how much could be made from a small amount of scenery. Our examination of the imported version found two compressed terrain sources, accompanied by separate radar maps. The later return phase reuses the first terrain with local changes, rather than loading a third wholly independent landscape. Repeated tile runs keep the stored maps compact; the level setup adds prisoners and alters selected cells. Revisiting familiar territory becomes part of the mission's structure, not simply a repetition of a static picture.

Its rough edges are revealing too. In a reproducible situation, an enemy helicopter below the player climbs through solid rock. More seriously, destroying the fort while prisoners remain inside can drive the remaining-prisoner count below zero and prevent the level from ending. These are observations of the C64 program, not stories about what its authors intended. They show how a convincing rescue adventure can depend on fragile bookkeeping beneath the action.

Hales later described early videogames as a form of short storytelling, and Fort Apocalypse makes that idea tangible without much written narrative. Fuel, prisoners, restricted passages and the journey back give the flight a beginning, a purpose and consequences. Its compact world asks the player to remember places and return to them under changed circumstances. The helicopter is a vehicle for that little adventure as much as it is a weapon.
