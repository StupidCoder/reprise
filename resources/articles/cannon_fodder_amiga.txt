The soldiers in Cannon Fodder are tiny, but losing one can feel remarkably personal. A few pixels become a name, a record of service and eventually a grave. That tension between cheerful presentation and lasting loss is central to the game. Its battlefield is immediately approachable, yet the campaign remembers what happened there long after the shooting has stopped.

Jon Hare traced the idea to several different periods of his life. There was a childhood battlefield drawn on a table with Chris Yates, the action of Rambo: First Blood Part II, and the team's more recent enthusiasm for Lemmings. Controlling a group rather than a lone hero brought those influences together. The resulting game needed both direct action and coordination.

It also marked a change inside Sensible Software. Julian Jameson led the programming and Stoo Cambridge the artwork, with Hare directing the design. In his interview with Gary Penn, Hare described unusually methodical preparation: squared-paper maps and a table of features that introduced something new as the campaign progressed. Quicksand and thin ice could fill corresponding roles in different environments. Variety was planned across the whole game.

Cambridge's recollections add the less glamorous side of that process. He described the creative freedom Hare gave him, but also the exhaustion of finishing Cannon Fodder after roughly two years of work and repeated play. Sending the masters to Virgin brought relief. For about a year afterward, he said, he could hardly bear to look at it. The apparent lightness of those little soldiers concealed a demanding production.

The names helped turn that production into the game itself. Hare told Arcade Attack that the list began with members of the team: Jops, Jools, Stoo and RJ. Later entries became increasingly invented. He also described the anti-war message as strong by the end of development, and felt that hostile press coverage had missed it. This is his account of the intended tone; the discomfort of enjoying the combat remains something the player encounters directly.

Our research into the Amiga original shows how much work supports that discomfort. Soldiers persist as campaign entries with names, ranks and service information. New recruits replace losses, while the hill, briefing and debriefing screens connect one battle to the next. Missions draw on separate maps, placements and terrain attributes. The small figures are therefore participants in a continuing campaign, rather than interchangeable lives that reset when a screen ends.

The game makes that distinction without interrupting every battle to explain it. Moving and firing are immediate; the consequences accumulate between missions. You may begin by counting the number of soldiers available and later recognise which ones are missing. The same small scale that makes the battlefield easy to read leaves room for the campaign to make each survivor familiar. That is where its playful appearance acquires a much sharper edge.
