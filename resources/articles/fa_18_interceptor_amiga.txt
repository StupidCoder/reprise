F/A-18 Interceptor makes a carrier deck feel very small. The open sky invites you to accelerate and experiment, then a landing asks for concentration and restraint. Its appeal lies in that movement between spectacle and control. Bob Dinnerman brought the instincts of an arcade programmer to a home-computer flight simulation, creating a place where learning to fly was already an absorbing game.

Dinnerman had worked on Discs of Tron at Bally/Midway before becoming fascinated by 3D graphics and flight simulation. In a 2004 interview with Tom Steinberg, he recalled seeing high-end simulation demonstrations at SIGGRAPH in 1984. Contacts made there led to an Amiga development opportunity. He bought an Amiga 1000 and a small demonstration subsequently won Electronic Arts' interest.

Contemporary reporting fills in another part of the journey. Amiga Computing described Interceptor as his first commercial home-computer game, developed over about a year and a half. Some flight work grew from an abandoned Bally project. The article also reported a division between assembly language for most of the program and C for menus and setup, a practical combination of speed-sensitive work and more conventional interface code.

The progression gave that simulation an approachable shape. Demonstrations, practice flying, prescribed manoeuvres and carrier qualification came before the missions. Dinnerman told the magazine that the F/A-18 offered a modern, single-seat carrier aircraft; the F-16 added variety and connected to a mission involving defectors. The aircraft selection thus served both the player's training and the scenarios built around it.

Dinnerman's later recollections include a particularly good example of a shipping decision. The ability to land upside down on the carrier was noticed close to completion. The team debated correcting it, but worried about disturbing the physics so near release. He was ultimately pleased they left it. He also remembered constructing the Golden Gate Bridge with graph paper and entering the model's numbers by hand: a recognisable landmark assembled one coordinate at a time.

Our examination of the original supports the broader picture of a purpose-built simulation. The interface and flight engine are distinct parts of the program, and the cockpit combines stored artwork with changing instruments and a calculated outside view. The sound system responds to events in flight rather than merely playing an unchanging background. Together, these layers make the cockpit a place that reports what the aircraft is doing.

That relationship between feedback and action gives the game much of its personality. A view outside the aircraft can clarify a manoeuvre that felt bewildering from the seat; returning to the instruments makes the same movement something to control. The scenery is spare enough to read, yet specific enough to invite exploration. Interceptor gives the player both a set of missions and a machine worth learning for its own sake.
