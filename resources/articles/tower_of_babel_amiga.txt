Tower of Babel puts three machines into a world that looks almost like a model on a desk. Their jobs are easy to name: zap, push and grab. The difficulty comes from arranging those abilities in the right order, often while another part of the puzzle is moving. Pete Cooke's Amiga game turns solid 3D scenery into something you can reason about, manipulate and eventually build for yourself.

The game's story mixes the biblical tower with visiting aliens and robotic helpers. Contemporary reviews describe the resulting adventure as a succession of smaller towers, each with its own objectives and available robots. That structure makes the grand setting manageable. Instead of learning an enormous landscape at once, you face a bounded problem with a particular set of tools, then carry the experience into another arrangement.

Those tools extend beyond direct control. You can give the robots short programs and let them act while you attend to another member of the team. Amiga Computing's July 1990 review highlighted this ability to run all three simultaneously. The attraction is easy to see: the player begins as an operator and becomes a choreographer, preparing actions that must make sense together.

Our examination of the original confirms how concrete those little programs are. Each robot has up to eight command positions; movements still obey the same space and obstacle rules as direct orders. An impossible command can be skipped. Programming therefore does not remove uncertainty from the tower. It lets you express a plan, then watch that plan meet the changing physical situation around the robots.

The included tower designer takes the idea one step further. It edits four floors, their objects, colours and objectives, and stores them in the same compact tower format used by the supplied content. In our checks, unpacking and repacking the original records preserved them exactly. For players, the important result is simpler: the construction kit works with the game's actual puzzle vocabulary, including the constraints that make a tower playable.

The presentation supports that model-building quality. The original draws the scene as solid geometry, with a camera, ground shadows and a separate sequence of sky effects. Beneath the view is a much smaller logical grid that decides where things can move. CU Amiga praised both the 3D and the designer, while Amiga Format awarded 95 percent. Reviewers did not agree on every aspect of the presentation, but the scope for puzzling and construction was unmistakable.

This is an unusually generous design to hand to the player. Understanding a tower means learning how its objects interact; creating one means using that same knowledge in reverse. The built-in editor makes the transition explicit. Long before sharing a custom level became an online routine, Tower of Babel let a completed puzzle become the starting point for making a new one.
