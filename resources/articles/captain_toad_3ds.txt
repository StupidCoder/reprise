Captain Toad's most important ability is something he cannot do: jump. In a Mario game, a low wall or a gap often invites a quick leap. Here, it can turn the whole route into a puzzle. Producer Koichi Hayashida explained that the team wanted compact, self-contained stages, but agile characters made those spaces difficult to design. Removing the jump let a much smaller world become a substantial adventure. The backpack gave that restriction a character of its own.

The familiar story that Captain Toad began as a Zelda game needs a little care. In a 2014 Nintendo Life interview, Hayashida described early Super Mario 3D World experiments with miniature stages and Mario. Director Shinya Hiratake proposed using Link, but that idea stopped during planning. The team eventually chose the backpack-wearing explorer from Super Mario Galaxy and included his stages in 3D World. After that game's completion, Miyamoto asked them to make a standalone Captain Toad game, fulfilling an ambition they had already held.

The little worlds were physical objects before players could turn them on a screen. Hayashida told GameSpot that Hiratake made clay models of stage ideas at home and brought them into work to help explain the design to Miyamoto. Finding the right hero also changed familiar dangers. Without a jump, enemies became more threatening. Hiratake described giving Shy Guys forward-facing awareness so that sneaking behind them could offer another way through. A modest movement vocabulary encouraged observation instead of constant confrontation.

A promising restriction was not enough to sustain an entire game. Hiratake recalled that the team made more than a hundred stages near the start of the standalone project and played them together. Walking alone began to feel repetitive, so pulling things from the ground became another useful action: a way to uncover treasure and interact with the world. The adventure's episodes then gave the designers room to develop that vocabulary, moving from an introduction through more involved mechanisms to demanding challenges. Small stages did not have to mean a small range of ideas.

Even the viewpoint was open to experiment. In Nintendo's Miiverse interview, Hiratake recalled trying first-person play across every stage. Looking out through Captain Toad's eyes was appealing, but the controls and rules became too complicated. The idea survived in more limited settings, including minecart sections and turnip cannons. That is a revealing compromise: the team could briefly let you enter its miniature worlds while retaining the outside view that made their routes and hidden corners readable.

The 3DS edition was more than an afterthought alongside the Switch release. In a 2018 interview with Polygon, reproduced by Nintendo Everything, Hayashida said the team had wanted stereoscopic depth while making the Wii U game, and initially planned a 3DS port. Work on Super Mario Odyssey then helped demonstrate the appeal of sharing controls with another player. A Switch version offered a way to revisit a two-player idea that had not made the Wii U release. According to Hayashida, the decision to produce both versions grew from those different opportunities.

Nintendo released the 3DS version on 13 July 2018, presenting its depth effect and touch controls as ways to put a miniature garden in the player's hands. Stereoscopic viewing required a compatible 3DS; the game also ran in 2D on the 2DS family. That distinction suits the underlying design. Depth could strengthen the illusion of a tiny place, but the pleasure still came from studying it, changing your view and discovering that an apparently impossible journey had a route all along.
