Bruce Lee puts a martial-arts star in a fortress, then makes the fortress as important as the fighting. Lanterns draw you across ledges, ladders offer escape or ambush, and a familiar room can change after you have done the right thing. It is a small cast moving through a carefully arranged sequence of spaces. Ron Fortier and artist Kelly Day had already worked together on the Atari conversion of Zaxxon before creating this very different adventure.

Day described researching architecture and trying to bring visual ideas from films into the game. That ambition had to survive the coarse shapes and limited colours of a home computer. The result suggests places rather than illustrating them in detail: a roofline, a hanging lantern or a stretch of patterned wall can give a room its identity. The scenery also has to remain readable while three characters pursue, dodge and strike one another.

One of the game's best ideas arrived through play. In a contemporary interview, Day recalled Fortier experimenting with joystick control of Green Yamo after Bruce's moves were working. Letting a second person take the enemy's part turned an obstacle into a role. The same ladder could now be guarded by someone who understood exactly where you wanted to go. It was a way of adding a second player's personality without adding a second hero.

The journey from Atari to Commodore has its own intriguing recollection. In a later Retro Gamer interview, Fortier described the C64 as the first conversion and said he chose to write an "Atari emulator". That is his wording, not a verified specification of the machinery. It is tempting to translate it directly into a modern picture of one complete computer running inside another, but the interview does not establish that. It is evidence of how he remembered adapting the Atari game, while the precise boundary between shared game logic and platform support still needs investigation.

Our C64 research gives a firmer view of what the finished version actually does. Its rooms are not stored as full-screen pictures. A compact stream mixes runs of repeated cells with stretches of individually specified cells, and the drawing system expands them using shared character graphics. Each stored map cell supplies a taller piece of the visible room. Repeated masonry and platforms therefore serve two purposes: they establish the fortress's visual language and make its rooms economical to store.

The enemies expose a similarly useful separation. The reconstructed behaviour shows that their decisions produce directional and attack inputs which feed the character movement machinery. A human-controlled Yamo can use that machinery too. This does not prove the order in which Fortier designed those systems, but it helps explain why the joystick experiment was such a productive one: the enemy already had a body capable of moving and fighting; changing who made its decisions changed the experience.

The fortress is more than a compressed backdrop. The C64 also runs room-specific logic alongside the shared movement and combat systems, so a chamber can have its own changing hazards and progress conditions. Reusing the actors while giving individual rooms distinctive behaviour is a large part of the game's variety. Between Day's economical scenery, Fortier's room logic and the unexpected human opponent, a small collection of components becomes a place worth learning rather than merely a succession of fights.
