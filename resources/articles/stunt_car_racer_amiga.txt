A racing game can be frightening without putting anything in your path. Stunt Car Racer makes the edge of the road enough: a narrow ribbon of track rises above empty ground, and every crest asks whether you have judged your speed correctly. Its unlikely shape grew from an experiment, rather than a plan to reproduce a real motorsport. Geoff Crammond recalled starting with a vehicle crossing rough, randomly generated terrain, possibly for a tank combat game.

Then he drove over a ramp. Taking off was more entertaining than the activity he had originally imagined, so the jumps became the point. He flattened the surrounding ground and added ramps, but finding and lining up with them was awkward. Joining them with a track solved that problem; banking the bends kept the emphasis on jumps rather than conventional cornering. In his account, the design emerged through driving and changing the prototype, not from a finished specification.

There was a considerable commitment behind that apparent simplicity. Crammond described more than two years of work and uncertainty about whether a new concept would succeed. He later added serial-link racing to the Amiga and Atari ST versions because he wanted a human opponent. That addition gave the precarious circuits another kind of pressure: someone else could be taking the same risk just ahead of you.

The Amiga edition also carries a less visible surprise. Our examination of its program found structures that strongly suggest a close conversion of an 8-bit, 6502-style core into 68000 instructions. Small values are still handled a byte at a time, and pairs of bytes are assembled into pointers in the opposite order from the Amiga processor's usual convention. The data retains a compact address scheme rather than consistently adopting the new machine's wider one. These are architectural fingerprints, not something a player would see in a screenshot.

It would go too far to call the conversion purely mechanical. The same examination found operations adapted to the 68000's larger arithmetic, while the Amiga has its own drawing, sound and loading machinery. Nor have we compared this image directly with the C64 executable. The defensible conclusion is that the Amiga program preserves substantial 6502-style organisation; an exact account of the conversion process would require more evidence. Moving to a more powerful computer did not necessarily mean discarding a working design.

The circuits reveal another economy. In the imported data, a course is assembled from a grid of cells, a small vocabulary of plan shapes and height profiles. Those ingredients describe the road's direction and its rises and falls. Read this way, the rollercoaster is not an enormous hand-built picture: it is a compact set of instructions for constructing a surface. The stored representation makes the changing height of the road as fundamental as its route.

That is a useful way to return to the driver's seat. The game's spectacle comes from the relationship between a simple-looking course and the vehicle moving over it. Its development story runs from discovering a pleasurable jump to organising a whole game around that sensation; its surviving data shows how economical the resulting world could be. The dramatic part is what happens between the road and the wheels, not how much scenery surrounds them.
