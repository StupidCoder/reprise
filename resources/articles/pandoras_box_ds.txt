Pandora's Box nearly sent its gentleman detective somewhere quite different. Hino told Wired that the planned second title concerned a ghost island, but the team reconsidered the idea of Layton struggling to survive on a deserted island. A changed premise produced the adventure known in North America as Diabolical Box. He also said the sequel deliberately connected puzzles more closely to their story situations. The change was about what suited Layton, as well as where to send him.

The finished cartridge shows the practical work of joining a question to an adventure. A room describes its exits, objects and hidden discoveries, while events manage conversations and changes in progress. The question does not exist alone: the game has to remember what led into it and what should happen afterwards. Our research finds that coordination expressed through both event scripts and the records that preserve the player's discoveries.

The questions were worked on collaboratively. Hino described residential puzzle workshops with Tago and the development team. A printed brainteaser and a touch-screen activity ask different things of a player: the latter must explain how to move, draw or submit an answer as well as what the problem is. Looking at the finished game, much of its apparent ease comes from keeping those two kinds of understanding close together.

Our cartridge research reveals considerable rebuilding beneath the familiar presentation. Curious Village kept most of its program resident together; the sequel divides much of its work into separately loaded modules. Rooms, events, films and puzzle types can take turns occupying memory. Some earlier puzzle machinery remains present without being used by this game's questions. This is evidence of a developing code base, not proof that the team planned particular discarded puzzles or removed them late in production.

The side activities show how much specialised work a comfortable little diversion can require. Tea blending combines three ingredients, compares the mixture with a recipe table and feeds the result back into a character's request. Camera repair uses its own placement and rotation rules, then opens a different activity built around photographs. The cartridge links these systems through progress records and events, making separate diversions part of the same continuing journey.

The hamster is more elaborate still. Arranging objects on a small board gives way to a three-dimensional walk, with rules for choosing a route, noticing food and reacting to toys. Our examination finds decisions about nearby paths and different actions for eating, jumping and playing. The animal's performance is thus a little simulation for the player to influence, rather than a film that simply runs after the board is filled.

Pandora's Box develops the series through that combination of continuity and new construction. The detective remains recognisable, but the machinery supporting his adventure is reorganised and the rewards ask for fresh kinds of attention. The abandoned island idea makes the process especially interesting: a sequel's identity can emerge as much from deciding what its hero would not do as from inventing another mystery for him to solve.
